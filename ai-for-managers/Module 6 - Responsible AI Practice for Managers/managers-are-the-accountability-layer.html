Accountability

Why Managers Are the Accountability Layer for AI

It is tempting to treat AI ethics as someone else's department: legal, HR, the AI governance committee. But policy that never reaches the daily workflow changes nothing, and the person who decides whether it reaches the workflow is the manager.

The Gap

Principles Exist. Practice Does Not.

Research finds that AI ethics principles are regularly deprioritized at the manager level precisely because they are not built into daily workflows. The gap between what senior leadership wrote and what teams actually do is real and persistent. That gap is yours to close.

What you already own

You see the actual tasks. You set the norms. You model the habits. You are the only person positioned to turn a principle into a daily practice on your team.

What changes when you own it

Responsible AI shifts from a checklist you comply with into a leadership responsibility you exercise, the same way you own your team's output and wellbeing.

Reza's story

Reza's company rolls out a one-page "Responsible AI Principles" document. It is fine, and nobody changes anything, because principles on a page do not touch the work. Reza is the one who turns "protect employee privacy" into a concrete team rule ("no employee names in public AI tools, ever") and "maintain human accountability" into a verification norm. The policy existed before Reza. It only became real on the team because Reza operationalized it. That is the accountability layer in action.

Apply It Today

Find the gap and close one of them

Find your organization's AI policy or principles (or note that none exists). Identify one principle that is currently not operationalized on your team, and decide one concrete practice that would make it real.

Reflect

  • Where is the biggest gap between your org's stated AI principles and your team's actual practice?
  • What would change on your team if you treated AI governance as your responsibility rather than someone else's?
